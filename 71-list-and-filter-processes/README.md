# List and filter processes

THEORETICAL RECAP On Linux, a process is a running instance of a program, identified by a unique PID (Process ID) and linked to its parent through a PPID. Each process has an owner (the user who launched it), a state (Running, Sleeping, Zombie, Stopped), and consumes resources such as CPU and memory. The correct mental model is a family tree: init/systemd (PID 1) is the root, and every other process descends from it. Killing a parent can orphan or terminate its children. To inspect processes, you have two families of tools. Static snapshots come from 'ps' (for example 'ps aux' or 'ps -ef'), which lists every process at a given instant. Dynamic, live views come from 'top' and 'htop', which refresh continuously and let you sort by CPU or memory. Filtering is where the real skill lies: 'ps aux | grep sshd', 'pgrep -u root', or 'ps -eo pid,user,%cpu,%mem,comm --sort=-%cpu' let you isolate exactly what matters. Common mistakes: confusing 'ps aux' (BSD syntax) with 'ps -ef' (System V syntax) and mixing their flags; forgetting that 'grep' itself appears in its own output; and ignoring zombie/defunct processes that signal a bug. A mini example: 'ps -eo pid,ppid,user,stat,comm | grep Z' reveals every zombie on the system, its PID, and its parent responsible for reaping it. In a security context, spotting an unexpected process running as root, or a hidden process consuming resources, is often the first sign of compromise.
